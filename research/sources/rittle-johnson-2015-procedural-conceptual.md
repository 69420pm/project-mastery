# Rittle-Johnson, Schneider & Star (2015). Not a one-way street: Bidirectional relations between procedural and conceptual knowledge of mathematics

Educational Psychology Review, 27, 587–597. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9302-x

- **Design and population:** narrative review of longitudinal and experimental studies, almost all preschool to middle school (counting, arithmetic, fractions, decimals, equation solving)
- **Intervention vs. comparison:** (1) whether procedural and conceptual knowledge predict and cause gains in each other; (2) studies that vary the type or order of instruction on concepts and procedures
- **Outcome and delay:** varies by study; mostly immediate post-tests, some across school years
- **Effect:**
  - **Longitudinal:** cross-lagged panel data show symmetric prediction both ways, for example in middle-school equation solving (Schneider et al. 2011)
  - **Experimental:** a brief procedure lesson improved concept knowledge and vice versa (p. 590)
  - **Ordering:** the only study varying only the order (Rittle-Johnson & Koedinger 2009, sixth-grade decimals in a tutor, two classroom experiments) found that iterating between concept and procedure lessons gave equal conceptual and greater procedural knowledge than concepts first (p. 592)
- **Boundary conditions:**
  - Practice supports concepts only when it is "carefully constructed": conceptually sequenced problems built both kinds of knowledge, while the same problems in random order gave only modest procedural gains (pp. 590–591)
  - A few lab studies favour starting with a brief concept lesson (p. 593)
  - No study compared concepts-then-procedures with procedures-then-concepts (p. 591)
- **Quality notes:** non-systematic review by researchers central to the debate; clear about how thin the ordering evidence is
- **Transfer notes:** children, not university students; university math adds proofs and abstract structures that the review does not cover

> "Evidence indicates that the relations between conceptual and procedural knowledge are often bidirectional, with improvements in procedural knowledge often supporting improvements in conceptual knowledge as well as vice versa." (p. 594)

> "we could not find empirical evidence to directly evaluate claims for an optimal ordering of instruction" (p. 591)
