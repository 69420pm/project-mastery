# Koedinger, Corbett & Perfetti (2012). The Knowledge-Learning-Instruction framework: Bridging the science-practice chasm to enhance robust student learning

Cognitive Science, 36(5), 757–798. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2012.01245.x

Read in the authors' final manuscript (figshare); page numbers below are manuscript pages, not journal pages. Sections 1–5 read closely, section 6 (worked examples) skimmed.

- **Design and population:** theoretical framework built on in vivo experiments of the Pittsburgh Science of Learning Center, "primarily from studies at the junior high school through college levels" (p. 5) in math, science and second-language courses
- **Intervention vs. comparison:** not applicable; three linked taxonomies: kinds of knowledge components (KCs), kinds of learning events, kinds of instructional events
- **Outcome and delay:** not applicable; the framework defines "robust learning" as learning that lasts (long-term retention), transfers, or accelerates future learning (pp. 5–6)
- **Effect:** none of its own. Table 6 (p. 28) marks which instructional principles have experimental support for which KC kind (facts, rules, principles); most cells hold one or two "+" and several are empty
- **Boundary conditions:**
  - **Definition of a KC:** "an acquired unit of cognitive function or structure that can be inferred from performance on a set of related tasks" (p. 9). Everyday terms such as concept, principle, fact or skill are all KCs. Many KCs sit at the "unit task" level of about 10 seconds, such as applying one theorem in a proof (p. 9).
  - **Grain size:** analyse a course "only on a single level that lies just above the level at which novices have achieved success and fluency" (p. 10); lower levels are treated as atomic only if the population has mastered them
  - **Kinds of KC (Table 3, p. 12):** constant–constant (facts, including definitions of terms in math, p. 13), variable-condition (categories, concepts, rules, procedures), and verbal KCs with a rationale (principles, theorems). "Integrative" KCs only show up as the difference between a harder task and its easier parts (pp. 15–16)
  - **Kinds of learning (Table 4, p. 23):** memory and fluency processes matter for every kind of KC; induction and refinement for rules and principles; sense-making only for principles. This is the "asymmetry hypothesis" (pp. 23–24, 28–29): simple instruction (spacing, retrieval, optimized scheduling) helps complex KCs too, but complex instruction (explanation, argumentation) does not help facts.
  - **Assessment depends on kind:** variable-condition KCs need varied assessment tasks before mastery can be inferred, because students induce over-specialised or over-general conditions (pp. 13, 17); integrative KCs need contrasting easy and hard tasks (p. 18)
  - **Expert blind spot:** experts cannot articulate much of their non-verbal knowledge, so expert task analyses miss KCs (p. 14). Example: teachers believed algebra equations were easier than word problems, but students did worse on equations (p. 7)
- **Quality notes:** framework paper, highly cited; the authors call their knowledge-by-instruction links "tentative hypotheses" that "all … require further testing" (p. 18). Useful for defining units and types, not as evidence of effect sizes
- **Transfer notes:** the source studies are close in material (secondary and college math and science) and use step-based tutors with immediate feedback; not LLM tutoring, and few delayed exams

> "a knowledge analysis for a particular course may focus only on a single level that lies just above the level at which novices have achieved success and fluency" (p. 10)

> "simpler learning processes (fluency and refinement) may support complex knowledge but complex learning processes (e.g., argumentation) may fail to support simple knowledge" (p. 23)
